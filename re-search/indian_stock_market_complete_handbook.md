# The Indian Stock Market and Trading: A Practical Complete Handbook

**India-focused edition for NSE and BSE**  
**Research date:** 27 July 2026  
**Audience:** A beginner who wants a serious foundation before risking money

> This is education, not a promise of profit, a trade call, or personal financial, tax, or legal advice. Rules, taxes, lot sizes, expiries, margins, and eligible contracts change. Check the latest exchange circular, broker contract specification, and a qualified professional before acting.

---

## 1. The most important truth first

The stock market is not a machine that pays people for predicting candles. It is a system for transferring ownership, raising capital, discovering prices, and transferring risk. A trader earns only when their advantage after all costs is greater than the combined effect of errors, randomness, and competition.

Trading is unusually difficult because a trade can be profitable even when the reasoning was bad, and unprofitable even when the reasoning was good. One result proves almost nothing. A real edge is a repeatable process that produces positive results over a large sample after brokerage, taxes, slippage, and mistakes.

The base rate should shape every beginner’s decisions. SEBI found that **93% of individual equity F&O traders lost money over FY22–FY24**, with aggregate losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore. A later SEBI study reported that about **91% lost money in FY25** and individuals had aggregate net losses of roughly ₹1.06 lakh crore. These are not warnings to ignore; they are the starting facts of the profession. [SEBI FY22–FY24 study](https://www.sebi.gov.in/media-and-notifications/press-releases/sep-2024/updated-sebi-study-reveals-93-of-individual-traders-incurred-losses-in-equity-fando-between-fy22-and-fy24-aggregate-losses-exceed-1-8-lakh-crores-over-three-years_86906.html) and [SEBI FY25 comparative study](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/jul-2025/1751900271726.pdf).

The sensible learning order is:

**cash-market investing → paper trading → small cash swing trades → carefully controlled intraday trading → derivatives only after demonstrated competence.**

Derivatives are not a beginner shortcut. Leverage makes both skill and mistakes larger.

## 2. What the market actually does

### 2.1 Primary and secondary markets

In the **primary market**, a company raises money by issuing securities. A fresh-share IPO sends new capital to the company. An Offer for Sale, or OFS, transfers existing owners’ shares and the sale proceeds go to those sellers, not to the company. A rights issue raises money from existing shareholders. Debt issuance raises borrowed capital.

In the **secondary market**, investors trade already-issued securities with one another on exchanges. If you buy one Reliance share on NSE, your payment normally goes through the clearing system to the seller; it does not go to Reliance Industries. The company benefits indirectly from access to capital, a market valuation, employee stock liquidity, and the ability to raise more capital later.

### 2.2 How money moves through the system

Suppose Buyer A places a limit bid for 100 shares at ₹500 and Seller B places a sell order at ₹500. The exchange matches them. The clearing corporation becomes the central counterparty, calculates obligations, collects margins where applicable, and coordinates funds and securities settlement. Depositories change the electronic ownership record.

This is the simplified chain:

**investor → broker → exchange order book → clearing corporation → bank and depository → investor**

NSE and BSE run markets. They do not hold your personal shares. India’s depositories, **NSDL and CDSL**, hold securities electronically, while a Depository Participant such as a broker or bank gives you access to the depository. You cannot open a retail account directly with NSDL or CDSL. [SEBI’s depository explanation](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/differencedp.html).

### 2.3 Why prices move

At the smallest scale, price moves when aggressive orders consume available opposite-side liquidity. If market buyers consume all shares offered at ₹100 and the next seller is at ₹100.10, the traded price rises. “More buyers than sellers” is an imprecise phrase because every completed trade has both. The useful distinction is:

**aggressive buyers versus passive sellers**, or **aggressive sellers versus passive buyers**.

At larger scales, urgency changes because of earnings, interest rates, policy, flows, index rebalancing, risk limits, news, expectations, hedging, and crowd positioning. Price reflects what participants expect about the future, not only what is true today.

### 2.4 Wealth creation versus trading transfer

A productive company can reinvest profits, grow, and distribute dividends. Long-term equity ownership can therefore create wealth rather than merely transfer it. A derivative contract is mostly a risk-transfer arrangement: before transaction costs, one side’s gain is the other side’s loss. SEBI explicitly describes exchange-traded derivatives as zero-sum before costs and warns that leverage can wipe out net worth. [SEBI derivatives guide](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/understanding_derivatives.html).

## 3. The Indian market ecosystem

### 3.1 Regulators and institutions

**SEBI** regulates the securities market, exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, merchant bankers, advisers, and many other intermediaries. **RBI** regulates banks, monetary policy, government debt infrastructure, and foreign-exchange rules. The **Ministry of Finance**, tax authorities, MCA, and other bodies have related roles.

**NSE and BSE** list securities and operate trading platforms. NSE is generally the dominant venue in equity derivatives; liquidity must still be checked contract by contract. BSE is the older exchange and hosts equities, indices such as SENSEX, and derivative products.

The same company can be listed and traded on both exchanges, but NSE and BSE have separate order books. A price difference can briefly exist; sophisticated arbitrage and common information usually keep liquid prices close. Your order on one exchange does not directly execute against the other exchange’s book. Compare the executable bid/ask, depth, charges, and broker routing—not only LTP. BSE’s principal equity-index derivative names include SENSEX and BANKEX, while NSE’s include the NIFTY family. Each exchange can have different contracts, liquidity, expiry schedules, strikes, and lots. SEBI’s official curation page links the separate [NSE and BSE option chains and derivative data](https://www.sebi.gov.in/curation/equity_derivatives.html).

**Clearing corporations** guarantee and settle eligible exchange trades, calculate obligations, collect collateral, and manage default risk. This central counterparty structure greatly reduces bilateral counterparty risk, but it does not remove market loss, gap, liquidity, broker, operational, or fraud risk.

**NSDL and CDSL** maintain dematerialised ownership records. Your **demat account** stores securities; your **trading account** sends orders; your **bank account** moves cash.

### 3.2 Market participants

Retail traders are only one group. Other important groups include mutual funds, insurance companies, banks, pension funds, Alternative Investment Funds, Foreign Portfolio Investors, proprietary trading firms, high-frequency firms, market makers, arbitrageurs, promoters, corporate treasuries, hedgers, and the government or RBI in relevant markets.

“FII” is still common market slang, but **FPI** is the formal modern category. **DII** is an informal umbrella for domestic institutions such as mutual funds and insurers.

Big players do not share one opinion. A mutual fund can buy while an insurer sells; an option market maker may sell calls but immediately buy futures as a delta hedge. A large visible order may be execution, inventory management, arbitrage, or hedging—not a directional prediction.

### 3.3 Banks and investment bankers

Commercial banks provide payments, custody, credit, treasury operations, forex services, and sometimes broking or investment services through regulated entities. Their trading desks manage liquidity, interest-rate, currency, and balance-sheet risks.

In Indian securities regulation, the IPO-facing investment-bank role is usually called a **merchant banker** or **Book Running Lead Manager (BRLM)**. It helps structure an issue, perform due diligence, prepare disclosures, coordinate regulators and intermediaries, build the order book, determine the issue price with the issuer, arrange underwriting when applicable, and oversee allotment-related responsibilities. A merchant banker does not guarantee that an IPO is a good investment. [SEBI Merchant Bankers Regulations, amended through February 2025](https://www.sebi.gov.in/legal/regulations/feb-2025/securities-and-exchange-board-of-india-merchant-bankers-regulations-1992-last-amended-on-february-10-2025-_92447.html).

### 3.4 “Operators”

“Operator” is market slang, not a regulated profession. It usually means a promoter-connected group, wealthy trader, or coordinated accounts believed to influence a stock. Some large trading is legitimate. Manipulative practices—circular trading, pump-and-dump promotion, spoofing, false appearances of volume, front-running, insider trading, or price rigging—are illegal.

You cannot prove an “operator” from one candle, an order-book wall, or a Telegram message. Warning signs include poor fundamentals with extraordinary price appreciation, repeated upper circuits, low free float, concentrated volume, promotional messages, unexplained preferential allotments, promoter pledging, related-party complexity, auditor resignations, and surveillance action. Check exchange filings, shareholding patterns, pledges, corporate announcements, ASM/GSM lists, and SEBI orders.

ASM and GSM are surveillance frameworks, not automatic declarations of guilt. Restrictions can include higher margins, trade-for-trade settlement, reduced price bands, and periodic call auctions. [SEBI surveillance links](https://www.sebi.gov.in/curation/surviellance.html) and [NSE GSM explanation](https://www.nseindia.com/static/regulations/graded-surveillance-measure).

## 4. Instruments you can encounter

### 4.1 Equity shares

A share is a fractional ownership claim on a company. Its value depends on future cash generation, assets, capital structure, governance, growth, risk, and the valuation the market assigns. Shareholders may receive dividends and voting rights, but dividends are not guaranteed and equity is residual: lenders are paid before owners in liquidation.

Useful categories include large-, mid-, small-, and micro-cap; growth, value, cyclical, defensive, turnaround, and compounder. These labels describe tendencies, not guaranteed behaviour.

### 4.2 Indices

An index is a rules-based basket used as a benchmark. You cannot buy an index number directly; you can use an index fund, ETF, futures, or options.

The **NIFTY 50** tracks 50 large NSE-listed companies and is free-float market-cap weighted. As of 30 March 2026 it represented about 53.73% of NSE-listed free-float market capitalisation. Higher free-float market cap means larger index influence. [NIFTY 50 official page](https://www.niftyindices.com/indices/equity/broad-based-indices/nifty--50).

The **BSE SENSEX** is a 30-stock benchmark. Other important families include NIFTY Bank, NIFTY Financial Services, NIFTY Next 50, sector indices, mid-cap and small-cap indices, strategy indices, and total-return indices.

A **price-return index** reflects price changes. A **total-return index (TRI)** assumes dividends are reinvested and is the fairer long-term performance benchmark.

Index movement can be concentrated. A heavily weighted bank can move NIFTY more than several small constituents combined. Always distinguish index breadth from index level.

### 4.3 ETFs, mutual funds, bonds, REITs, and InvITs

An **ETF** trades on exchange and usually tracks an index, commodity proxy, or bond basket. Check tracking difference, liquidity, spread, expense ratio, underlying liquidity, and creation mechanism.

A **mutual fund** pools investor money. Active funds select securities; passive funds track indices. It is usually more suitable than active trading for people without a tested edge or sufficient time.

**Bonds and government securities** are debt claims. Main risks are interest-rate risk, credit risk, liquidity risk, reinvestment risk, and inflation. Bond price and yield generally move in opposite directions.

**REITs** and **InvITs** provide exchange-traded exposure to income-producing real estate and infrastructure. Distribution yield is not the same as a fixed deposit rate and unit prices can fall.

### 4.4 Commodities

Commodity derivatives include bullion, energy, base metals, and agricultural products. NSE and BSE have commodity segments, but Indian commodity liquidity is often associated with **MCX** and agricultural contracts with **NCDEX**. If your rule is “NSE/BSE only,” product choice and liquidity will be narrower.

Commodity futures are not simply stocks with longer hours. They have contract-specific units, quality standards, delivery centres, tender periods, expiry rules, storage and logistics effects, and often physical-delivery obligations. SEBI’s framework says physical delivery is the preferred settlement mode unless justified otherwise. Never hold a commodity contract near delivery without reading its current specification. [SEBI commodity derivatives master circular](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/aug-2023/1691151096694.pdf).

Drivers include global spot markets, USD/INR, inventories, weather, geopolitics, OPEC decisions, freight, seasonality, local duties, and basis.

### 4.5 Currency derivatives

Exchange-traded currency contracts cover INR pairs such as USDINR, EURINR, GBPINR and JPYINR, plus permitted cross-currency pairs. They are standardised, margined contracts, not the same thing as buying cash dollars.

RBI’s current Master Direction states that INR-involving exchange-traded foreign-exchange derivatives are for hedging contracted exposure. A user may take positions up to the prescribed aggregate threshold without presenting evidence upfront, but must still have a valid unhedged contracted exposure and be able to establish it if asked. Do not interpret “no document upfront” as permission for unrestricted speculation. [RBI Master Direction](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx_id%3D10485%281%29.html).

Currency prices respond to relative inflation and interest rates, trade flows, capital flows, risk sentiment, RBI intervention, crude-oil imports, and global dollar strength.

### 4.6 Futures

A futures contract obliges the long and short to settle a standardised exposure at expiry. You deposit margin rather than full notional value.

If NIFTY futures are at 24,000 and the lot size were 75, one contract controls ₹18,00,000 notional. If required margin were ₹2,00,000, a 1% move in the underlying changes contract value by about ₹18,000—roughly 9% of posted margin before costs. The lot and margin are examples, not current quotes.

Futures are marked to market daily. Profits and losses affect available funds. Stock futures in India can create physical-settlement obligations at expiry; index derivatives are cash settled. Verify current settlement rules with the exchange and broker.

**Basis = futures price − spot price.**

Futures can trade above spot because of financing cost minus expected income, or below it due to dividends, demand, or positioning. Basis converges toward zero near expiry.

**Rollover** means closing the expiring contract and opening a later one. It is two trades with spread, basis, cost, and execution risk.

### 4.7 Options

A call gives the buyer a right linked to upside; a put gives the buyer a right linked to downside. The buyer pays premium and has no further loss beyond premium for a simple long option. The seller receives premium but takes a contingent obligation and may face very large loss.

At expiry:

`Call intrinsic value = max(spot − strike, 0)`  
`Put intrinsic value = max(strike − spot, 0)`

Before expiry:

`Option premium = intrinsic value + time value`

An option buyer can correctly predict direction and still lose because the move was too small, too late, or implied volatility fell. An option seller can be profitable frequently and still suffer a rare loss larger than many prior gains.

## 5. Accounts, orders, matching, and settlement

### 5.1 Opening the right accounts

Use a SEBI-registered broker and DP. Complete KYC using your own PAN, bank, email, and mobile. Read tariff sheets, risk disclosures, policies, and optional authorisations. Enable exchange and depository alerts. Never share OTP, PIN, password, API secret, or remote access.

The DP holds securities; the broker routes trades. They may be the same company but the roles are distinct.

### 5.2 Order types

**Market order:** execute immediately against available prices. It prioritises execution, not price. Dangerous in illiquid instruments.

**Limit order:** execute only at the stated price or better. It controls price but may not fill.

**Stop order / stop-loss order:** activates after a trigger. A stop-market seeks execution and may slip; a stop-limit can remain unfilled during a gap.

**IOC:** fill immediately as much as possible, then cancel the remainder.

**Day order:** remains until filled, cancelled, or the session ends.

Broker labels such as CNC, delivery, intraday, MIS, cover, or bracket are broker facilities, not universal exchange concepts. Product availability and auto-square-off rules vary.

### 5.3 Price-time priority and the order book

Indian exchanges use anonymous electronic order books. Better price has priority; among orders at the same price, earlier time normally has priority. [NSE’s order-driven market description](https://www.nseindia.com/static/products-services/equity-market-trading).

The **bid** is the best displayed buying price and the **ask** is the best displayed selling price. The difference is the **spread**. Market depth shows resting quantities at multiple levels.

Displayed liquidity is not guaranteed. Orders can be cancelled, hidden beyond available depth, split across venues, or replenished by algorithms. A large bid can be genuine support, an execution order, or bait. Treat depth as conditional evidence.

### 5.4 Slippage and market impact

Slippage is the difference between expected and actual execution. It grows with volatility, order size, spread, low liquidity, news, and market orders. Market impact is the price movement caused by your own trade.

A backtest that assumes every entry at the candle close and ignores spread is usually overstated.

### 5.5 Settlement

NSE Clearing currently follows **T+1** rolling settlement for the normal equity cash market, with optional **T+0** settlement for eligible securities and participants. A T+1 trade made Monday normally settles Tuesday if both are working settlement days. In T+0, the eligible trade settles the same day. [NSE settlement cycle](https://www.nseindia.com/static/products-services/equity-market-settlement-cycle).

An intraday broker position is normally closed before settlement and produces only cash P&L. A delivery purchase creates a securities receivable. Selling shares you do not deliver creates a short delivery and can lead to auction and close-out cost.

### 5.6 Short selling

Intraday cash short selling is available subject to broker and exchange rules, but an overnight naked cash short is not permitted. Delivery must be arranged, commonly through the **Securities Lending and Borrowing Scheme (SLBS)**. Futures provide another way to hold short exposure but add leverage, basis, expiry, and margin risks. [SEBI securities-market FAQ](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/docfiles/20615_t.html).

### 5.7 Market sessions

As of the cited NSE schedule, normal equities and equity derivatives trade **09:15–15:30 IST**. Equity has a regular pre-open beginning at 09:00. Currency and commodity hours differ, and commodity closing time varies with US daylight saving. Verify holidays and special sessions each day. [NSE market timings](https://www.nseindia.com/static/market-data/market-timings).

Typical equity intraday character:

The open often has overnight price discovery, wide spreads, and high volatility. Midday often becomes slower and more mean-reverting. The close can have institutional execution, index flows, expiry effects, and position reduction. These are tendencies, not rules.

The Indian session is influenced by earlier Asian trading, European opening during the afternoon, GIFT Nifty, overnight US markets, global futures, crude, USD/INR, and scheduled domestic events. A foreign market correlation can reverse when local information dominates.

## 6. Trading styles

### 6.1 Investing

Time horizon is years. The investor studies business quality, management, financial statements, valuation, industry structure, capital allocation, and portfolio fit. Volatility is tolerated when the thesis remains valid; permanent capital loss is the main danger.

### 6.2 Swing trading

Positions last several days to months. Swing traders seek trends, breakouts, pullbacks, catalysts, relative strength, or mean reversion. Overnight gaps are unavoidable. Position size must be small enough that a gap through the stop does not damage the account.

### 6.3 Intraday trading

All positions are closed the same day. This removes overnight risk but increases noise, costs, screen pressure, and the temptation to overtrade. Intraday edge can come from opening range behaviour, trend continuation, VWAP interaction, event reactions, statistical reversion, or order-flow imbalance.

### 6.4 Scalping

Scalping seeks small moves over seconds to minutes. It demands excellent execution, low latency and costs, tight risk control, liquid products, and emotional discipline. A strategy earning 4 ticks gross but losing 3 ticks to spread, slippage, and charges has almost no edge.

Scalping is not “easy because stops are small.” Small stops are hit more often, and transaction costs consume a larger share of expected profit.

### 6.5 Position trading

This sits between swing trading and investing. Positions may last weeks or months and use macro, fundamental, trend, or carry views.

### 6.6 Arbitrage and market making

Arbitrage tries to capture relative mispricing, such as cash-futures basis or option parity differences. Real arbitrage requires simultaneous execution, funding, borrowing, margins, taxes, and operational reliability.

Market makers quote both sides, manage inventory, and hedge. They earn spread and related economics but carry adverse-selection, inventory, volatility, and technology risk.

## 7. Price action and market structure

Price action is the interpretation of raw price behaviour in context. It is not a collection of magic candle names.

### 7.1 Trend and structure

An uptrend generally makes higher swing highs and higher swing lows. A downtrend makes lower highs and lower lows. A range rotates between boundaries.

Define swings objectively using a timeframe or pivot rule. Otherwise a trader redraws structure after seeing the outcome.

A **break of structure** is meaningful only relative to a defined swing. A **change of character** is a possible early transition, not proof of reversal.

### 7.2 Support, resistance, supply, and demand

Support is an area where buying previously became sufficient to stop a decline. Resistance is where selling stopped a rise. Supply and demand zones are broader versions often based on an impulsive departure from a base.

These are zones, not exact laser lines. They matter because of trapped positions, resting orders, memory, valuation interest, hedging, and attention. Repeated testing can either validate a zone or consume its available liquidity.

What the user called “demand” twice is important enough to distinguish:

**economic demand** means willingness to buy at various prices; a **chart demand zone** is only an inference from prior price behaviour. The chart does not reveal all underlying demand.

### 7.3 Breakouts and false breakouts

A breakout is acceptance beyond a reference such as a range high. Quality improves when the break has participation, closes beyond the level, holds on retest, aligns with higher-timeframe structure, and has room before the next barrier.

A wick beyond a level followed by immediate rejection is often called a false breakout or liquidity sweep. It may trap late entrants, but sometimes it is merely noise.

### 7.4 Liquidity

Liquidity has several meanings:

**market liquidity** is the ability to trade size with little price impact. **Funding liquidity** is access to cash or credit. **Displayed liquidity** is resting book quantity. **Latent liquidity** is unshown willingness to trade.

A “liquidity pool” in discretionary trading usually means an area where many stops or breakout orders may cluster: above equal highs, below equal lows, around previous-day extremes, round numbers, range edges, or major swing points.

A **liquidity sweep** is a move through such an area followed by rejection. Do not assume every wick is institutional stop hunting. Stops can cluster naturally, and price can pass through because genuine information changed value.

### 7.5 Imbalance and fair-value gaps

An imbalance is a rapid one-sided move with limited two-way trade. Some traders mark a three-candle “fair-value gap.” Markets often revisit such zones, but there is no obligation to fill them, and definitions vary. Test an exact rule before using it.

### 7.6 Multi-timeframe analysis

Higher timeframes provide context; lower timeframes provide execution. A common hierarchy is daily for regime, hourly for structure, and 5- or 15-minute for entry. Too many timeframes create contradictions. Predefine which timeframe controls the thesis and which controls the stop.

## 8. Candles and chart patterns

A candle contains open, high, low, and close for a chosen interval. The body is open-to-close; wicks show extremes. The same order flow can look different on 1-, 3-, or 5-minute charts, so pattern names are timeframe-dependent.

### 8.1 Common candle patterns

| Pattern | Simple meaning | What must be checked |
|---|---|---|
| Doji | Open and close are close; temporary balance | Location, prior trend, following confirmation |
| Hammer | Small body with long lower wick after decline | Whether buyers hold the recovery |
| Hanging man | Hammer shape after an advance | Needs bearish confirmation |
| Shooting star | Long upper wick after an advance | Resistance, volume, next candle |
| Bullish engulfing | Up body covers prior down body | More useful after decline at support |
| Bearish engulfing | Down body covers prior up body | More useful after rise at resistance |
| Inside bar | Range contracts inside prior bar | Volatility compression, breakout direction |
| Outside bar | Range exceeds prior bar | Expansion; close determines message |
| Marubozu | Large body with little wick | Strong one-sided auction, but can exhaust |
| Morning/evening star | Three-candle reversal sequence | Context and gap definitions in 24-hour markets |
| Tweezer top/bottom | Similar highs/lows across candles | Often too subjective alone |

No single pattern is a trade. “Bullish engulfing at weekly support after a downside liquidity sweep with sector strength” is a contextual thesis. “Bullish engulfing anywhere” is a shape.

### 8.2 Larger patterns

Triangles, flags, wedges, head-and-shoulders, double tops/bottoms, cup-and-handle, and rectangles describe compression, continuation, or attempted reversal. Their names do not create edge. Define entry, invalidation, target, volume condition, and failure behaviour.

A head-and-shoulders can fail and become a strong continuation. Pattern failure itself contains information because traders positioned for the obvious outcome must exit.

## 9. Indicators: what they measure and what they do not

Indicators transform price or volume. They do not add information unless the transformation helps a decision.

### 9.1 Moving averages and EMA

An SMA equally weights the last `n` prices. An EMA weights recent prices more heavily:

`EMA today = α × price today + (1 − α) × EMA yesterday`  
where `α = 2 / (n + 1)`.

EMA slope and price position can describe trend. A fast/slow crossover is late by construction and whipsaws in ranges. “Price above 20 EMA” is a state, not a reason to buy.

### 9.2 RSI

The Relative Strength Index compares average gains and losses over a lookback and ranges from 0 to 100. Traditional labels call above 70 overbought and below 30 oversold.

Overbought means strong recent upside, not “must fall.” In a strong trend RSI can stay elevated. Useful applications include regime, failure swings, pullbacks to trend-specific zones, and divergence. Divergence warns that momentum changed; it does not time a reversal.

### 9.3 VWAP

Volume-Weighted Average Price is cumulative traded value divided by cumulative volume, usually reset each session. Institutions may use it as an execution benchmark. Price above VWAP indicates trading above the session’s volume-weighted mean.

VWAP bands can support mean-reversion or trend frameworks. VWAP is most meaningful in liquid instruments with reliable volume.

### 9.4 ATR

Average True Range measures recent range including gaps. It measures volatility, not direction. ATR helps normalise stops and position size:

`position quantity = rupee risk / stop distance`

If the stop is 2 ATR away, size must be lower than with a 1 ATR stop.

### 9.5 Volume

Volume is the number of shares or contracts traded. It measures participation, not whether “buyers are stronger,” because every trade has both sides.

High volume at a breakout can support acceptance. High volume with no progress may show absorption. Low volume pullbacks can be constructive in trends. All interpretations require location and subsequent response.

### 9.6 RVOL, not “ROVL”

The requested term “ROVL” is likely **RVOL—Relative Volume**:

`RVOL = current volume / typical volume for a comparable period`

For intraday use, compare 10:00 a.m. volume with normal volume by 10:00 a.m., not with an entire day. RVOL of 2 means roughly twice normal participation for the comparison.

### 9.7 Open interest

Open interest, or OI, is the number of outstanding derivative contracts. Volume counts trading activity; OI counts contracts remaining open.

The common table—price up/OI up = long buildup, price down/OI up = short buildup, and so on—is only an inference. Every new contract has a long and short. OI cannot identify whose directional conviction is “correct.”

### 9.8 CVD and order-flow delta

Trade delta estimates aggressive buy volume minus aggressive sell volume. **Cumulative Volume Delta (CVD)** sums that delta.

`CVD = cumulative(ask-lifted volume − bid-hit volume)`

If price rises while CVD falls, traders may infer passive absorption or data mismatch. But CVD quality depends on accurate trade-side classification and consolidated data. Retail Indian broker feeds may not provide authoritative aggressor flags or all exchange events. Do not treat synthetic tick-rule CVD as ground truth.

### 9.9 OBV, MACD, Bollinger Bands, ADX, and India VIX

OBV accumulates volume by close direction. MACD measures relationships among EMAs and is trend/momentum oriented. Bollinger Bands express price relative to a moving mean and standard deviation; a band touch is not automatically a reversal. ADX estimates trend strength, not direction.

**India VIX** is an exchange-calculated expectation of near-term NIFTY volatility derived from option prices. It is not a direction forecast. Rising VIX usually means options are pricing more uncertainty; falling VIX can hurt long-option positions through vega.

### 9.10 Indicator redundancy

EMA, MACD, and RSI all derive from price. Using all three does not necessarily provide three independent confirmations. Prefer a small set representing different dimensions: structure, volatility, participation, and execution.

## 10. Order flow and auction-market thinking

Order flow asks how market orders interact with resting liquidity. Key concepts are bid/ask, spread, depth, executed volume, absorption, exhaustion, imbalance, and replenishment.

**Absorption** occurs when aggressive orders trade repeatedly but price makes little progress because passive liquidity replenishes. **Exhaustion** occurs when aggressive participation fades. **Initiative activity** pushes price away from value; **responsive activity** trades against an excursion toward perceived value.

A footprint chart groups executed bid/ask volume by price. A DOM ladder shows resting depth. A heatmap shows how displayed liquidity changes over time. These tools can help, but they remain incomplete:

orders can be cancelled; iceberg liquidity may be hidden; related markets influence price; option market makers hedge in futures; one broker’s feed may aggregate or omit events; and colocation firms react faster than a retail screen.

Use order flow to refine a pre-existing location and risk plan, not to invent certainty from every flicker.

## 11. Futures and options in depth

### 11.1 Leverage and margin

Leverage is:

`notional exposure / your capital committed`

Margin is collateral, not maximum loss. A futures loss can exceed the original margin. Brokers and clearing corporations can increase margin during volatility. Forced liquidation may happen at an unfavourable price.

Option buyers pay premium upfront. Option sellers post SPAN/exposure and other margins. A hedged spread may receive margin benefit only when all legs are recognised; execution of one leg first creates temporary naked risk.

### 11.2 Option moneyness

For calls, strikes below spot are ITM; above spot are OTM. For puts, strikes above spot are ITM; below spot are OTM. ATM is nearest the current underlying.

ITM options have more intrinsic value and usually higher delta. OTM options are cheaper in rupees but often have a lower probability of expiring ITM. “Cheap premium” can still be expensive relative to its probability.

### 11.3 Greeks

**Delta** estimates option-price change for a small underlying move and is also used as a rough sensitivity/probability proxy. Call delta is positive; put delta negative.

**Gamma** measures how delta changes. It is often highest near ATM and expiry. High gamma makes near-expiry options react violently.

**Theta** estimates time decay, all else equal. It accelerates near expiry, especially for ATM options, but is not collected smoothly each minute.

**Vega** measures sensitivity to implied volatility. Long options are generally long vega; short options short vega.

**Rho** measures interest-rate sensitivity and is usually less important for short-dated equity options.

Greeks change continuously and depend on a pricing model. They are estimates, not contractual guarantees.

### 11.4 Implied and realised volatility

Realised volatility describes actual past movement. Implied volatility, or IV, is the volatility input that reconciles an option model with market premium.

Options are relatively expensive when IV is high compared with plausible future realised volatility, but “high IV” alone is not a sell signal. It may correctly price an event or crash risk.

**IV crush** is a sharp fall in IV after uncertainty resolves, often after results or policy events. A long straddle can lose despite movement if the movement is smaller than priced.

### 11.5 Volatility smile and skew

Different strikes have different IV. Equity-index downside puts often trade at higher IV because crash protection is valuable. Compare IV across strikes and expiries, not only one ATM number.

### 11.6 Option chain

An option chain displays calls and puts across strikes for an expiry: bid, ask, LTP, volume, OI, OI change, IV, and sometimes Greeks.

Read it in this order:

1. Confirm underlying and expiry.
2. Locate spot/futures and ATM.
3. Check spreads and volume.
4. Compare IV across strikes.
5. Examine OI and changes, but separate new positions from closing.
6. Check event and expiry context.
7. Use Greeks to understand sensitivity.

Large call OI is not automatically resistance; it may be covered calls, a spread leg, market-maker inventory, or hedging. Large put OI is not automatically support. OI changes after price moves are descriptive and can reverse quickly.

The NSE option-chain page states that volume and OI are shown in contracts and highlighted options are ITM. [NSE option chain](https://www.nseindia.com/option-chain).

### 11.7 Option book versus option chain

“Option book” can mean two things. The **order book** is the live bid/ask depth for one option contract. A trader’s **option position book** is the collection of option legs and their combined Greeks and payoff. The option chain is the cross-strike table.

Never judge a multi-leg book one leg at a time. Aggregate delta, gamma, theta, vega, margin, expiry payoff, and gap scenarios.

### 11.8 Put-call ratio

PCR is commonly put OI divided by call OI, or put volume divided by call volume. It is context-dependent and not a universal contrarian signal. Define which PCR, expiry, and history you use.

### 11.9 Max pain

Max pain is the expiry price at which aggregate option-holder intrinsic payout would be smallest under simplifying assumptions. It ignores entry price, closing trades, hedging, and participant motives. It is not a magnet law.

### 11.10 Expiry and settlement

As of NSE’s specifications updated 10 February 2026, NIFTY 50, NIFTY Bank, NIFTY Financial Services, NIFTY Midcap Select, NIFTY Next 50, and individual-security derivatives expire on the relevant **Tuesday**, or the previous trading day if Tuesday is a holiday. NIFTY 50 has weekly options; several other NSE indices are monthly-only. Contract cycles and expiry rules can change. [Current NSE contract specifications](https://www.nseindia.com/static/products-services/equity-derivatives-contract-specifications).

Do not memorise old Thursday-expiry tutorials. Verify the actual contract symbol and exchange circular.

Stock-derivative expiry can lead to physical settlement, creating delivery or funding obligations. Brokers may raise margins or close positions before expiry. Index derivatives are cash settled.

### 11.11 Common option strategies

| Strategy | View | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Long call | Bullish, volatility helpful | Premium can go to zero |
| Long put | Bearish/insurance | Premium can go to zero |
| Covered call | Mildly bullish/neutral on held stock | Upside capped; stock downside remains |
| Protective put | Own stock plus downside insurance | Premium drag |
| Bull call spread | Moderately bullish | Limited loss and profit |
| Bear put spread | Moderately bearish | Limited loss and profit |
| Short put spread | Bullish/neutral | Gap loss up to defined maximum |
| Long straddle | Large move either way | Theta and IV crush |
| Short straddle | Small move expected | Large tail/gamma risk |
| Iron condor | Range expected | Gap and volatility expansion |
| Calendar spread | Term-structure/volatility view | Complex Greeks and event risk |

“Defined risk” does not mean small risk. A trader can repeatedly lose the full defined amount.

## 12. Hedging

Hedging reduces a specific risk by accepting a cost or giving up some upside. It does not create free protection.

### 12.1 Equity hedge examples

A diversified portfolio can be partly hedged with NIFTY puts or short NIFTY futures. The hedge ratio depends on portfolio beta:

`futures contracts ≈ portfolio value × beta / futures contract notional`

A portfolio of small caps is not perfectly hedged by NIFTY because basis and correlation can break. A bank-heavy portfolio may be better matched to a banking index, subject to product eligibility and liquidity.

A protective put defines downside below a strike but costs premium. A collar buys a put and finances part of it by selling a call, sacrificing upside.

### 12.2 Hedge effectiveness

Measure hedge P&L against portfolio P&L, not whether the hedge itself made money. A hedge losing during a rising market may be working as insurance.

Main hedge risks are wrong size, wrong underlying, expiry mismatch, basis risk, liquidity, theta cost, margin change, and dynamic delta drift.

### 12.3 Diversification is not the same as hedging

Diversification reduces idiosyncratic concentration by owning different risk sources. A hedge adds an offsetting exposure. Ten highly correlated financial stocks are ten names but one dominant risk.

## 13. Risk management: the actual core of trading

### 13.1 Risk per trade

Choose the maximum account loss if the stop executes normally:

`rupee risk = account equity × risk percentage`

`quantity = rupee risk / (entry − stop + expected slippage per unit)`

Example: ₹2,00,000 account, 0.5% risk = ₹1,000. Entry ₹250, stop ₹245, assumed slippage ₹0.50. Quantity is `1000 / 5.5 ≈ 181`, rounded down for lot/tick constraints.

For options, size from the option’s planned stop or worst-case premium loss—not from underlying points alone. For overnight trades, stress a gap beyond the stop.

### 13.2 R-multiples

Define initial risk as 1R. A ₹1,000 planned loss is −1R; a ₹2,000 gain is +2R. This makes performance comparable across trades.

`expectancy = win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss`

A 40% win-rate system can be profitable if winners are sufficiently larger than losses. A 90% win-rate short-option system can fail if rare losses are enormous.

### 13.3 Drawdown and risk of ruin

A 50% drawdown requires a 100% gain to recover. Risk should shrink when equity falls, not rise in desperation. Set daily, weekly, and strategy-level loss limits.

Do not risk money required for rent, emergency funds, debt repayment, education, or near-term goals. Never borrow to trade.

### 13.4 Stops

A stop belongs where the thesis is invalidated, then position size adapts. Moving a stop farther solely to avoid taking a loss increases risk after the market has disagreed.

Types include structure stop, volatility/ATR stop, time stop, and portfolio stop. Broker-side stops can fail from gaps, illiquidity, trigger rules, or outage; always have an emergency procedure.

### 13.5 Pyramiding

Pyramiding adds to a winning position as the thesis confirms. It differs from averaging down.

A safe framework is to define total maximum risk before entry, add only after favourable movement, reduce each successive add, recalculate the common stop, and never let total open risk exceed the cap. Adding because unrealised profit “is house money” is faulty; it is your money.

### 13.6 Correlation and sector constraints

Position risk cannot be added independently when trades correlate. Long HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Bank NIFTY, and a financial ETF is one concentrated financial-sector bet.

A practical portfolio policy might include:

`single-position risk ≤ 0.5% of equity`  
`total open risk ≤ 2%`  
`single-sector open risk ≤ 1%`  
`no more than two positions driven by the same catalyst`  
`gross and net market beta monitored`

These numbers are examples, not personal advice.

Use a rolling correlation matrix, but also run stress scenarios because correlations rise during crises. Distinguish **sector diversity** from **factor diversity**: several sectors can all be long high-beta growth.

### 13.7 Liquidity constraints

Limit position size relative to typical traded volume and visible depth. Avoid orders large enough to create material impact. Illiquid options can show theoretical profit that cannot be exited near the displayed LTP.

### 13.8 Operational risk

Prepare for internet failure, broker outage, exchange halt, rejected order, duplicate click, stale quote, API bug, wrong quantity, wrong expiry, and physical settlement.

Keep broker and exchange contact details, a backup connection, position alerts, and a written kill-switch process. Confirm positions from the order/trade book, not only a chart.

## 14. Sector analysis, correlation, and breadth

Stocks are influenced by market, sector, industry, and company factors. A bank depends on rates, credit growth, margins, asset quality, and regulation; an IT exporter depends on foreign demand, billing, utilisation, attrition, and currency; an oil marketing company has crude and policy sensitivity.

**Relative strength** compares a stock or sector with a benchmark. A stock rising 1% while its sector rises 3% is up absolutely but weak relatively.

**Breadth** asks how many stocks participate. Useful measures include advance-decline, percentage above moving averages, new highs/lows, and sector participation. An index rising on a few heavyweights while breadth falls is narrower, but it can remain narrow for a long time.

Sector rotation often reflects the economic and rate cycle, but the sequence is not fixed. Use earnings revisions, valuation, price strength, breadth, and macro sensitivity rather than a rigid rotation diagram.

Diversification constraints should be written before positions are opened. Examples include maximum stock weight, sector weight, small-cap exposure, illiquid exposure, correlated cluster risk, and event risk.

## 15. Fundamental and macro analysis

### 15.1 Financial statements

The income statement shows revenue, expenses, and profit. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity. The cash-flow statement reconciles cash from operations, investing, and financing.

Important measures include revenue growth, operating margin, return on equity, return on capital employed, free cash flow, debt/equity, interest coverage, working-capital days, dilution, and contingent liabilities.

Profit without cash conversion deserves investigation. Related-party transactions, promoter pledges, qualified audit opinions, frequent auditor/CFO changes, and complex subsidiaries can be governance risks.

### 15.2 Valuation

P/E is price divided by earnings per share. P/B compares price with book value. EV/EBITDA relates enterprise value to operating earnings. Dividend yield compares dividend with price. DCF estimates present value of future cash flows.

No ratio says “cheap” alone. A low P/E can reflect decline; a high P/E can be justified only if growth, quality, and duration support it. Compare with history, peers, cycle, and realistic scenarios.

### 15.3 Corporate actions

Dividends transfer value from company cash to shareholders, so price adjusts around ex-date. A stock split changes unit count and price, not underlying enterprise value. A bonus issue similarly changes shares outstanding without creating free economic value. Buybacks, rights issues, demergers, mergers, preferential allotments, and ESOP dilution require case-specific analysis.

### 15.4 Macro drivers

Monitor RBI policy and liquidity, inflation, GDP, fiscal policy, bond yields, crude oil, USD/INR, global rates, foreign flows, domestic fund flows, and earnings. The market responds to the difference between outcome and expectation. “Good news” can produce a fall if better news was priced in.

Use an event calendar for RBI decisions, Union Budget, results, elections, major court/regulatory decisions, index rebalances, expiry, and global central banks.

## 16. IPOs

An IPO can be a fresh issue, OFS, or combination. Read the DRHP/RHP, especially risk factors, objects of issue, use of proceeds, capital structure, promoter background, litigation, related parties, financials, peer comparison, and basis for offer price.

In book building, investors bid within a price band. Retail applicants can bid at cut-off. **ASBA** blocks funds in the bank; money remains in the account until allotment and only the allotted amount is debited. UPI is an available ASBA mechanism for eligible applications. [SEBI ASBA guide](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/ipo_through_asba.html) and [SEBI book-building guide](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/book_building.html).

Subscription multiples do not guarantee listing gains. Grey-market premium is unofficial, unregulated, and manipulable. Anchor participation is not a recommendation. SEBI review of an offer document is not approval of the investment quality.

Evaluate post-issue market cap, enterprise value, dilution, fresh capital versus seller exit, customer concentration, cash conversion, adjusted profits, and lock-ins. SME IPOs can have severe liquidity and market-making risks.

## 17. Manipulation, fraud, and unsafe practices

Avoid guaranteed-return claims, paid tip groups without proper registration, account handling by strangers, APK files, screen sharing, fake broker apps, “institutional account” schemes, and requests to send money to personal accounts.

**Dabba trading** records a bet outside a recognised exchange. It is illegal and provides no exchange grievance or clearing protection. [SEBI dabba-trading warning](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/dabba_trading.html).

Common abuses include pump and dump, insider trading, front-running, circular trading, wash trades, spoofing/layering, marking the close, misleading corporate disclosures, and social-media impersonation.

For grievances, first approach the intermediary, then the exchange/depository as appropriate, and use SEBI SCORES/ODR. Preserve contract notes, bank records, emails, screenshots, order IDs, and complaint numbers. Insist on a contract note within the prescribed timeline and reconcile exchange/depository alerts. [SEBI investor do’s and don’ts](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/securities-dos_and_donts.html).

## 18. Costs and Indian taxation

### 18.1 Trading costs

Net P&L must include brokerage, Securities Transaction Tax, exchange transaction charges, SEBI charges, GST on eligible service charges, stamp duty, DP charges for delivery debits, slippage, impact, borrowing cost, interest, and data/software costs.

Delivery equity usually attracts STT on both purchase and sale. Intraday equity has a different STT treatment. Derivative STT uses contract-specific taxable value.

From **1 April 2026**, STT on sale of securities options is **0.15% of option premium**, STT when an option is exercised is **0.15% of intrinsic value** payable as prescribed, and STT on sale of securities futures is **0.05% of futures trade value**. Delivery equity remains 0.1% on purchase and sale under the cited schedule. Verify changes before calculation. [NSE 2026 STT schedule](https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/content/circulars/FATAX73524.pdf) and [NSE STT page](https://www.nseindia.com/static/products-services/equity-derivatives-securities-transaction-tax).

These taxes can make frequent low-edge trading unviable.

### 18.2 Income-tax classification

Tax treatment depends on facts and classification. Delivery share activity may be capital gains or business income depending on conduct and consistency. Intraday non-delivery equity is generally treated as speculative business. Eligible exchange-traded derivatives on recognised exchanges are treated as **non-speculative business** under section 43(5), despite everyday use of the word speculation. [Income Tax Department section 43](https://www.incometaxindia.gov.in/w/section-43-16).

For listed equity subject to conditions, the current special-rate framework includes 20% under section 111A for relevant short-term gains and 12.5% under section 112A for relevant long-term gains above the statutory exemption threshold. Holding period, STT conditions, set-off, carry-forward, audit, turnover calculation, expenses, advance tax, and return form all matter.

Tax law changes and individual facts are decisive. Maintain broker ledgers, contract notes, P&L, turnover calculation, expense evidence, bank statements, and holdings from day one, and consult a Chartered Accountant experienced in securities trading.

## 19. Building a real trading process

### 19.1 Write a testable setup

A setup must specify:

**market and instrument; timeframe; regime filter; entry; stop; target/exit; size; time window; no-trade conditions; costs; and maximum simultaneous risk.**

“Buy when price action looks bullish” cannot be tested. “Buy first 15-minute close above prior-day high when 20-day RVOL is above 1.5 and sector index is above VWAP; stop below breakout bar; exit at 2R or 15:15” can be tested.

### 19.2 Backtesting

Use survivorship-free data when possible. Avoid look-ahead bias, future-adjusted constituents, unrealistic fills, and tuning many parameters to the past.

Split research into in-sample design, out-of-sample validation, and forward paper/live-small testing. Include spread, slippage, all charges, rejected/unfilled orders, corporate actions, and regime variation.

Important statistics are number of trades, expectancy, median outcome, win rate, average win/loss, profit factor, maximum drawdown, time under water, tail loss, turnover, exposure, and performance by regime.

### 19.3 Paper trading

Paper trading tests rules and workflow but not emotional or fill reality. Treat fills realistically at bid/ask, then move to the smallest practical live size. Scaling is a new experiment because execution and psychology change.

### 19.4 Journal

Record timestamp, instrument, screenshot, setup, regime, entry/stop/exit, size, planned risk, slippage, charges, result in R, maximum favourable/adverse excursion, rule adherence, and lesson.

Separate:

**strategy outcome**—did the setup work?  
**execution quality**—did you follow it?  
**process quality**—was the setup valid under the plan?

Do not change rules after three losses. Review statistically meaningful batches.

### 19.5 Daily operating routine

Before market: check holidays, events, corporate results, expiry, global context, gaps, levels, eligible instruments, margins, and maximum risk.

During market: trade only planned setups, use correct quantity and expiry, confirm orders, monitor total risk, stop at the daily limit, and avoid revenge trading.

After market: reconcile positions and contract notes, record costs and screenshots, journal, and back up data. Do not judge yourself only by today’s P&L.

## 20. Common myths corrected

**“More indicators mean more confirmation.”** Often they repeat the same price information.

**“A high win rate is a good system.”** Not without payoff and tail-loss data.

**“Option buying has limited risk, so it is safe.”** Repeated 100% premium losses can destroy an account.

**“Option selling is safer because most options expire worthless.”** Sellers can face negatively skewed, leveraged tail loss.

**“Stop-loss guarantees the stop price.”** Gaps and illiquidity create slippage.

**“Big OI is support or resistance.”** It reveals positions, not motives or permanence.

**“Institutions are buying, so price must rise.”** Reported flows can be hedged, delayed, or offset elsewhere.

**“Averaging lowers risk.”** It lowers average price while increasing exposure.

**“The market is manipulated, so analysis is useless.”** Manipulation exists, especially in weak/liquid names, but most losses still arise from no edge, leverage, costs, and poor discipline.

**“Price action predicts the future.”** It organises conditional probabilities and invalidation.

**“Backtest profit proves an edge.”** It may prove overfitting.

**“More leverage creates more return.”** It first creates more variance and a higher probability of ruin.

## 22. A sensible learning curriculum

### Stage 1: Market literacy

Learn accounts, orders, settlement, statements, corporate actions, costs, taxes, scams, and exchange rules. Observe cash equities without leverage.

### Stage 2: Analysis

Study business fundamentals, market structure, trends/ranges, support/resistance, volume, volatility, and position sizing. Build one setup.

### Stage 3: Evidence

Backtest it, paper trade it, include costs, and journal at least a meaningful sample across regimes.

### Stage 4: Small live execution

Use a size at which a full planned loss feels boring. Prove rule adherence before scaling.

### Stage 5: Derivatives

Only then learn futures notional and mark-to-market, option Greeks, IV, physical settlement, margin, spreads, and hedging. Use payoff graphs and stress tests before every multi-leg structure.

Progress should be based on process statistics, not a profitable month.

## 23. Compact pre-trade checklist

**Thesis:** What exact behaviour am I betting on, and why now?

**Context:** Trend, range, volatility, event, sector, breadth, and higher timeframe?

**Instrument:** Correct exchange, symbol, expiry, strike, lot, and settlement?

**Liquidity:** Spread, depth, volume, OI, and realistic exit?

**Risk:** Entry, invalidation, quantity, rupee risk, gap risk, total correlated exposure?

**Reward:** Is expected payoff positive after all costs?

**Execution:** Limit or market, trigger behaviour, partial-fill plan, outage plan?

**Psychology:** Is this planned, or FOMO/revenge/boredom?

If one answer is vague, the trade is not ready.

## 24. Core glossary

**Ask:** Lowest displayed selling price.  
**Bid:** Highest displayed buying price.  
**Beta:** Sensitivity to a market benchmark.  
**Basis:** Futures minus spot.  
**Breadth:** Participation across securities.  
**Broker:** Registered intermediary routing client trades.  
**CAGR:** Annualised compounded growth rate.  
**Circuit:** Price-movement restriction or market-wide halt.  
**Clearing:** Calculation and management of post-trade obligations.  
**Delivery:** Securities and funds exchanged in settlement.  
**Demat:** Electronic securities account.  
**Drawdown:** Decline from a prior equity peak.  
**Edge:** Positive expected outcome after costs over many trials.  
**Expiry:** Last trading/settlement date of a derivative contract.  
**Free float:** Shares broadly available for public trading, excluding strategic holdings.  
**Haircut:** Reduction applied to collateral value.  
**Impact cost:** Price effect of executing an order relative to an ideal price.  
**ISIN:** Unique security identifier.  
**Limit order:** Order at a specified price or better.  
**Liquidity:** Ability to transact without large impact.  
**LTP:** Last traded price; not necessarily an executable price.  
**Margin:** Collateral required for exposure; not a loss cap.  
**Market cap:** Share price multiplied by shares outstanding.  
**Mark to market:** Revaluation and settlement of position gains/losses.  
**Notional:** Total economic exposure controlled.  
**OI:** Outstanding derivative contracts.  
**Price band:** Allowed trading range under exchange rules.  
**R:** Initial planned risk per trade.  
**Settlement:** Final exchange of cash/securities or cash difference.  
**Short sale:** Sale intended to profit from decline, requiring closing or delivery.  
**Slippage:** Difference between expected and actual fill.  
**Spread:** Ask minus bid.  
**Tick:** Minimum price increment.  
**Turnover:** Trading value calculated under the relevant market/tax convention.  
**UCC:** Unique Client Code.  
**Volatility:** Magnitude and variability of price movement.

## Conclusion

Trading competence is not the ability to name dojis, RSI divergences, liquidity sweeps, or option strikes. It is the ability to define a small advantage, measure it honestly, size it so failure is survivable, execute it consistently, and stop when evidence says the edge is absent.

For an Indian beginner, the highest-value first steps are to understand cash-market mechanics, protect account access, learn financial statements and risk sizing, build one testable non-leveraged setup, and keep records. Futures and options should come later, when notional exposure, Greeks, volatility, settlement, tax, and tail risk are no longer abstract words.

The objective is not to trade every day. The objective is to remain solvent, informed, and selective long enough for genuine skill to matter.
